Compliant Leg Redesign

SCUTTLE, our lab's multi-segmented robot, moves by combining body undulation with rigid, C-shaped leg rotation. That works well on flat ground, but on rough terrain the rigid legs become a bottleneck: a tall obstacle can stall a leg mid-rotation, and a side impact makes it slip instead of clearing the obstacle, so the robot keeps actuating without making forward progress.

I'm leading the redesign of SCUTTLE's leg into a variable-stiffness compliant leg. The bottom 90° stance region keeps four rigid spokes for normal ground contact, while the side regions use thin, curved spokes that buckle elastically when they hit an obstacle, letting the leg deform around it instead of stalling or slipping. I'm building the compliant spokes out of TPU (thermoplastic polyurethane).

Next, I'm characterizing how spoke stiffness depends on three geometric parameters (spoke thickness, spiral curvature radius, and spoke width), and deciding between a single-material TPU print or a multi-material design with rigid PLA stance spokes and TPU compliant spokes.

First Iteration

My first printed prototype let me test the spoke geometry and flex behavior by hand before putting it on the robot.

Current Progress

I printed six legs for baseline testing, but the lab's SCUTTLE robot wasn't available, so my next step is learning to run it through its Raspberry Pi controller, which will also set me up for testing beyond this project.

SCUTL Steering Tests

In a separate project with Jai and Jianfeng Lin, I tested how SCUTL, our multi-segmented centipede robot, can steer through posture alone. A steering bias is added to the horizontal body joints, tapered with a cosine window across the front m joints so it fades out gradually along the body instead of ending abruptly. With body undulation turned off, the robot holds a fixed curved shape while its legs drive it forward, so any turning comes purely from that held shape.

We swept the bias magnitude β from -30° to +30° in 10° steps and the number of biased joints m across 1, 3, and 5, for 21 conditions with 3 trials each, to see how turning depends on both the size of the bias and how far back along the body it reaches.

The widest condition in the sweep, β = 30° applied to all five joints with a cosine taper (30, 27.1, 19.6, 10.4, 2.9° head to tail), curves the whole body rather than just the front, and turns like a fixed steering angle since there's no body wave to modulate it.

Averaging the start and end position of each condition across all 62 trials shows both parameters matter: larger bias magnitudes turn faster, and spreading the bias across more joints (larger m) increases the turn rate at a given β, confirming that both the size and reach of the postural bias shape how tightly SCUTL turns.

Centipede-Inspired Multi-Legged Locomotion

I'm also part of a larger robophysics study, led by Jianfeng Lin and presented at APS 2026, on how an elongated, multi-segmented robot modeled after centipedes coordinates its body waves and leg stepping to move efficiently. My role has been running experiments: testing different vertical and horizontal body-wave phase lags against the leg gait to see how they affect locomotion performance, work that fed into the group's effective-contact model and geometric-mechanics analysis of the robot's gait.
